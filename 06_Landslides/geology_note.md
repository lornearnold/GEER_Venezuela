# Literature note — regional geology of the source area

Background for §6.2, to fill the draft placeholder: *"The geology of the area is _brief description of
mountainous area with shallow soils_."* Covers the bedrock, the weathering/residual-soil profile, and
the slope character that together make these slopes chronically failure-prone. Companion to
[lit_review_1999.md](lit_review_1999.md), which covers the 1999 event and background landslide rates.

## The setting in one paragraph (drop-in draft text)

The landslides cluster on the north flank of the **Cordillera de la Costa** (Venezuelan Coastal Range),
the steep coastal mountain front that rises directly from the Caribbean to peaks over 2,000 m within a
few kilometers of the shore between Caracas and Caraballeda. The range is the exhumed core of an ancient
continental margin, built of **Precambrian–Paleozoic metamorphic rocks — chiefly schist and gneiss, with
subordinate quartzite and marble** (Larsen et al., 2001; Wieczorek et al.). Around Caraballeda the
dominant unit is the **San Julián Formation** (mica schist and gneiss of the Ávila Metamorphic Suite).
These rocks are pervasively **fractured and deeply weathered** in the humid tropical climate, producing a
mantle of **thin colluvium and residual soil (regolith), typically only ~0.5–2 m deep**, over the
fractured bedrock. That shallow, cohesionless mantle sits on **very steep slopes — commonly 30–60°, with
1999 scar sites averaging ~42°** — so it is easily saturated and mobilized as shallow debris slides and
flows during intense rain or strong shaking (Larsen et al., 2001).

## Details, by piece

**Tectonic/structural framing.** The Cordillera de la Costa is a stack of terranes and nappes assembled
by oblique collision between the Caribbean and South American plates since the Mesozoic; the range core
(the **Ávila Belt**) exposes rocks of a former passive continental margin that were metamorphosed and
later exhumed. The same active plate-boundary setting that raised the range (uplift on the order of a few
mm/yr) keeps the rock mass fractured and the relief steep — the structural reason the slopes stay primed
to fail. The June 2026 sequence sits on this boundary.

**Lithology.** Dominantly **quartz–albite–muscovite schist and quartz–chlorite schist, gneiss, and
metagranite**, with lesser quartzite, marble, and metaconglomerate (central Cordillera de la Costa is
Venezuela's most lithologically complex region, with up to ~12 mapped terranes). For §6.2 the useful
level of detail is just "fractured metamorphic schist and gneiss." Around the 1999/2026 impact zone the
mapped bedrock is the **San Julián Formation** and related Ávila Suite units (San Julián, Peña de Mora,
Colonia Tovar Gneiss, Guaremal Metagranite).

**Weathering & residual soil (the "shallow soils" of the draft line).** Warm, wet climate drives intense
chemical and physical weathering of the schist/gneiss, turning the fractured rock into a **regolith of
residual soil and colluvium** that is easily eroded and, critically, **thin** — on the steepest slopes
loose material can only accumulate to shallow depth before it fails and sheds downslope. The 1999 failures
were **shallow debris slides ~0.5–2 m deep in the weathered mantle over fractured schist/gneiss**
(Larsen et al., 2001) — i.e. the failure plane is at or just above the soil/rock contact, not deep-seated.

**Slope geometry.** Coastal front rises from sea level to >2,000 m over just a few km, giving sustained
slopes of **~30–60°**; a sample of 26 of the 1999 scars had a **mean hillslope angle of 42° (σ ≈ 7.6°)**
(Larsen et al., 2001). Most source-area failures are inside **El Ávila National Park**, undeveloped
forest, so the slopes themselves are close to natural condition.

**Why this matters for the trend in §6.2.** The combination — steep tectonic front + fractured
metamorphic bedrock + thin, supply-limited colluvial/residual soil mantle — is exactly what makes the
draft's "shallow failures" observation and the "1999 depleted the loose source material" hypothesis
physically reasonable: between big triggers, weathering slowly reloads a thin mantle on oversteepened
slopes, which the next storm or earthquake strips again (see the colluvium-supply-limited mechanism in
[lit_review_1999.md](lit_review_1999.md) §4).

### Confidence flags

- **Solid (primary, USGS):** shallow debris-slide depth (0.5–2 m), mean scar angle 42°, 30–60° slopes,
  schist/gneiss bedrock — all Larsen et al. (2001) / USGS FS-103-01.
- **Solid (regional geology literature):** San Julián / Ávila Suite lithology, terrane framing — Urbani
  and the Cordillera de la Costa nomenclature synthesis.
- **Approximate / for framing only:** the "~0.5–2 m" regolith depth is the *failure-plane* depth from the
  1999 slides, a reasonable proxy for typical mantle thickness but not a mapped soil-depth survey. Uplift
  "a few mm/yr" is order-of-magnitude (see [lit_review_1999.md](lit_review_1999.md) §4, ~2–5 mm/yr).
