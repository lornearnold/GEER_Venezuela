## 1. Regional geology of the source area

**Setting/relief** — Larsen et al. 2001 (FULL): headwater elevations of the affected catchments are
"2,000–2,700 m dropping to sea level over 6–12 km"; "An extremely steep, tectonically active
mountain front forms the boundary with a tropical sea." OFR (FULL): crest of the Sierra de Avila
reaches 2,700 m within 6–10 km of the coast.

**Lithology** — Wieczorek et al. 2001 OFR (FULL), Geology section, verbatim:

> "Exposures of the Tacagua Formation (Mesozoic)... consisting of graphitic, epidote schists
> parallels the coast and extend about 1 km inland."

> "Further inland... quartz-plagioclase-mica gneiss and feldspar schist of the San Julián Formation
> (Paleozoic), and augen (quartz-mica) gneiss of the Peña de Mora Formation (Precambrian)... extend
> to the crest of the Sierra de Avila."

Note: the OFR body never uses "Ávila Suite" (Urbani & Ostos 1989 "El Complejo Avila" appears only
in its reference list). The Ávila Metamorphic Suite nomenclature is from Urbani 2005
(**ABSTRACT only** — verify before leaning on it).

**Soil/regolith thickness** — OFR (FULL), verbatim:

> "The soils developed over these schists are relatively thin, 0.5- to 3.0-m thick (Salcedo,
> 2000)... The A-horizon is generally less than 0.3 m thick; however, bedrock weathering is
> commonly 2 m or more (Merifield, 2001)."

> "Most slides occurred within the top 0.5-2.0 m of soil and/or weathered schist or gneiss."

So: **soil mantle 0.5–3.0 m (per Salcedo 2000, secondhand); failure depth 0.5–2.0 m.** These are
two different numbers; earlier notes conflated them and misattributed both to Larsen et al. 2001
(which contains no geology or soil-thickness statements at all — only "debris flows of a few meters
or less in depth").

**Slope angles** — OFR (FULL), verbatim:

> "Measurements indicated that landslides initiated on slopes ranging from 30 to greater than 60
> degrees, and the slopes that failed had a mean value of 42 degrees with a standard deviation of
> +/- 7.7 degrees (N=26). Salcedo (2000) reported a mean initial slope value of 38.5 degrees with
> a standard deviation of +/- 5.1 degrees (N=15)."

Larsen et al. 2001 (FULL) has the same sample: "Hillslopes measured in a sample of 26 landslides
had a mean slope angle of 42º (standard deviation of 7.6º)." **σ differs: 7.6 (Larsen) vs 7.7
(OFR) vs I-2772's "mean value of 41° with a standard deviation of ±7.8° (N=26)".** Cite 42° with
Larsen et al. 2001 and don't sweat the tenths; the 30–>60° initiation range is OFR only.

USGS FS-103-01 (FULL) contains **no lithology and no numeric slope angles** — the earlier
geology note's attribution of "schist and gneiss on 30–60° slopes" to it was wrong.

## 2. The 1999 Vargas event

### 2.1 Rainfall

- Larsen et al. 2001 (FULL): "Rainfall accumulation at sea level on the Caribbean coast at the
  Maiquetía airport for the first 2 weeks of December was 293 mm, more than five times the average
  (MARN, 2000)." and "An additional 911 mm of rainfall was recorded from December 14-16."
- OFR (FULL), nails the gauge and window: "The total 3-day rainfall along the coast at the
  International Airport at Maiquetia (43 m above mean sea level) for a 52-h span on December 14-16
  totaled 911 mm"; "Hourly rainfall from 6 to 7 am on the morning of December 16 measured 72 mm";
  "the daily totals (380.7 and 410.4 mm) for both December 15 and 16 at Maiquetia exceeded the
  1000-yr probability rainfall event" (citing Martinez, 2000).
- FS-103-01 (FULL) gives a different antecedent window (not a conflict, different period):
  "Rainfall on December 2-3 totaled 200 millimeters (8 inches)".
- I-2772 (FULL pamphlet): 72 mm/h has "a 50-year (yr) return interval (Salcedo, 2000)"; with 1999
  included "the return period decreased to 270 yr (Bello and others, 2000)"; "Mean annual
  precipitation... 51 yr (excluding 1999) at Maiquetía... is 523 mm." (López & Courtel 2008 say
  "about 500 mm" and quote the storm as "900 mm... in these three days".)

### 2.2 Landslide character

Larsen et al. 2001 (FULL), verbatim:

> "Landslides caused by the rainstorm numbered in the thousands in the Cordillera de la Costa";
> "mainly debris flows of a few meters or less in depth but 100's of meters in length, and shallow
> soil slips, which were generally a few meters or less in thickness but, in many cases, 100's of
> meters in width"; "The majority of debris flows began as either shallow debris slides or soil
> slides (slips)."

> "Most of the landslide scars are on the north side of the mountain range including large areas
> in El Avila National Park."

Failure depth "top 0.5-2.0 m of soil and/or weathered schist or gneiss" is OFR (see §1). Landslide
width (OFR): mean 19.5 m, SD ±20 m.

### 2.3 Spatial extent

- Larsen et al. 2001 (FULL): "Flash floods and debris flows occurred in most of the several dozen
  small catchments (watershed areas on the order of 10 to 30 km2) that drain the Cordillera de la
  Costa"; "On virtually every alluvial fan along 50 km of coastline..."
- López & Courtel 2008 (FULL): "generated large debris flows and flash floods in about 24 streams
  that washed away many towns settled on the alluvial fans, along 50 km of the coastal zone."
- **Conflict:** OFR says "a 40-km coastal strip north of Caracas from La Guaira to Naiguita";
  I-2772 says "a 20-km (kilometer)-long coastal strip" (same endpoints!). Use ~50 km (Larsen;
  López) or report the disagreement.
- **The "~200 km² combined drainage area" figure appears in NO source read. Do not use.**
  (I-2772 Table 1 drainage areas, MARN 1999, for the ten listed catchments: Camurí Chiquito 10.2,
  San Julián 21.3, Cerro Grande 25.4, Camurí Grande 45.3, Alcantarilla 1.5, Seca 3.0, El Cojo 6.5,
  San José de Galipán 15.0, Osorio 4.7, Carmen de Uria 11.6 km² — sums to ~145 km² for those ten,
  which are the *examined* catchments, not all affected ones.)

### 2.4 Volumes and deposits

- **Event total** — I-2772 (FULL pamphlet): "López and others (2000) evaluated the cumulative
  volume of flood and debris-flow deposition along the coast in December 1999 as amounting to 20
  million m³ (M=7.3)." This is the only event-total volume in any source read (secondhand via
  I-2772; López et al. 2000 itself NOT READ).
- **The "sediment yield up to 100,000 m³/km²" figure appears in NO source read — not in López &
  Courtel 2008, not anywhere. Do not use.** (Formerly attributed to "López et al."; López &
  Courtel's only related sentence is qualitative: "The rapid process of sedimentation is
  associated to the large sediment yield capacity of the basins.")
- **Caraballeda fan (Río San Julián)** — sources disagree:
  - Larsen et al. 2001 (FULL): "Approximately 1.25 million m3 of debris flow deposits... were
    distributed across several square kilometers of fan surface" with "deposit thickness of up to
    4 m" including "numerous massive boulders up to 10 m long"; plus "An estimated 450,000 m3 of
    sediment was deposited in a 1,000-m wide subaerial delta with an average seaward extent of 50 m."
  - OFR (FULL): "Total depositional volume of 1.9 million cubic meters was determined from field
    measurements... the results show a depositional volume of 1.8 million cubic meters... both
    values differ by only approximately 10%." Deposits "between 4 and 5-m thick in the center of
    the fan"; "maximum thickness of deposits was 5.3 m"; I-2772 topo comparison "up to 7 m."
    Shoreline extended "by about 40 to 60 meters."
  - I-2772 abstract: "about 2 million cubic meters"; "Boulders up to 5 m long were carried along
    by the flows... and were deposited on the fan."
  - FS-103-01 caption (different unit): "an estimated 1.8 million tons of freshly deposited
    sediment."
  - **For the report: cite ~1.2–1.9 million m³ (Larsen et al., 2001; Wieczorek et al., 2001).**
    The Larsen 1.25 M m³ vs OFR 1.8–1.9 M m³ discrepancy is real and unexplained (possibly deposit
    subset vs total); the delta volume (450,000 m³) is Larsen only.
- **Boulders** — Larsen et al. 2001: "boulders as large as 10 meters in diameter" (general) and
  "up to 10 m long" (Caraballeda). OFR's largest single measured boulder: "Large (11.3 x 5.0 x
  3.5 m) sub-rounded gneissic boulder... Quebrada Camurí Chiquito" (in-channel). FS-103-01:
  "massive boulders as large as 10 meters (33 feet) in diameter... deposited in layers up to
  several meters thick."
- **Carmen de Uria** — Larsen et al. 2001 (FULL): "at the mouth of an 11.9 km2 watershed";
  "deposited a new subaerial delta of approximately 233,000 m3, assuming a 20 degree submarine
  slope. This volume of deposition indicates a minimum erosion depth of 20 mm averaged over the
  entire watershed." **Conflict:** I-2772 Table 1 lists Carmen de Uria at **11.6 km²** with a
  deposit volume of "(1.6)" ×10⁶ m³ attributed to López et al. (2000). (Delta ≠ total deposit;
  both can be true. Watershed area differs 11.9 vs 11.6.)
- **Fan loading** — Larsen et al. 2001: "massive amounts of new sediment were disgorged upon fan
  surfaces in quantities of up to 15 metric tonnes per square meter."

### 2.5 Deaths and damage

Sources genuinely disagree; report the range and say so:

| I-2772 2002 (FULL) | "The number of fatalities is estimated to have been about 19,000 (Larsen and others, 2001)." | — |

### 2.6 Historical recurrence

- Larsen et al. 2001 + OFR + FS-103-01 (all FULL, consistent): "at least one or two high-magnitude
  flash-flood or landslide events per century... since the 17th century." Larsen lists 19 dated
  events 1693–1963 plus 13 high-magnitude events in the 1970s. OFR names 1798, 1912, 1914, 1938,
  1944 (×2), 1948, 1951.
- Thunderstorm frequency — Larsen et al. 2001 (FULL): "Northern Venezuela lies within a region
  where an average of 50 thunderstorms per year are documented for any point on the land surface
  (Hayden, 1988)."
- **Uplift rate (formerly "~2–5 mm/yr"): in NO source read. Only "tectonically active." Do not
  use a number.**
- Prehistoric events — OFR: a 10-m-thick prehistoric debris-flow deposit bracketed by radiocarbon
  at ~3,720–4,270 yBP; prehistoric boulder sizes ≥ 1999's ("the December 1999 event was not
  necessarily the largest event").

### 2.7 Published maps of the 1999 event

I-2772 (FULL pamphlet): two sheets, 1:25,000. Sheet 1: extent of Dec 14–16 debris flows/flooding
in major channels of the nine examined drainages, "Mapped from aerial photographs and field
examination," plus GOES-8 rainfall contours. Sheet 2: pamphlet + Table 1 + **Caraballeda fan
only** deposit-thickness and max-boulder-size contour maps. There is **no range-wide source-scar
inventory** — the gap our own mapping fills. OFR examined nine watersheds by name (Camurí
Chiquito, San Julián, Cerro Grande, Camurí Grande, Alcantarilla, Seca, El Cojo, San José de
Galipán, Osorio).

## 3. Background landslide rates (reserved for a future paper)

Decision (Lorne, 2026-07-24): the GEER report treats background activity qualitatively (pre-event

### 3.1 Puerto Rico (discarded as lithologic analog; frequency framework still useful)

Larsen & Torres-Sánchez 1998 (**FULL**, re-verified from `larsen1998.txt` during this audit):

- Lithology of the three study areas — **no metamorphic rock**: Blanco = "Cretaceous
  marine-deposited volcaniclastic breccia," lavas, tuff + quartz-diorite intrusion; Cibuco =
  Oligocene–Miocene limestone (N) + volcaniclastics (S); Coamo = "Cretaceous and Tertiary
  volcaniclastic rock composed of tuffaceous sandstone, siltstone, breccia and" tuff.
- Units verbatim (Tables 8–11 captions): "frequency of landslides (number of landslides per km2
  per decade)".
- Land-use averages (slides/km²/decade), re-read from the tables: Blanco forest **0.8**,
  agriculture **2.5**, roads/structures **3.2**; Cibuco forest **1.4**, agriculture **2.6**, roads
  **11.8**; Coamo agriculture+forest combined **2.6**, roads **5.9**. (Lorne's 0.8–2.6 range =
  forest-to-agriculture averages.) Body text: "The frequency of landslides in agricultural regions
  of the Blanco and Cibuco study areas averaged 2 to 3 times the frequency determined for forested
  areas"; roads "ranged from 2.3 to 8.4 times" forested.
- Table 11 (all land uses, steep vs gentle): Blanco 4.5/1.9, Cibuco 16.3/8.3, Coamo 11.6/1.5.
- The lithology-is-marginal counterpoint, verbatim: "has been shown in a number of humid-tropical
  settings, lithology is often of only marginal importance because of the geotechnical
  similarities among weathered bedrock types (Pitts, 1992)."

### 3.3 Serra do Mar (closest physiographic + lithologic twin)

**No published chronic landslide frequency exists** — Cabral et al. 2021 (PARTIAL-A): F–M
relationships for Serra do Mar are "non-existent." Available instead: ¹⁰Be denudation ~0.08 mm/yr
steep oceanic flank (Gomes et al. 2022, ABSTRACT) and ~0.026 mm/yr southern segment (Salgado et
al. 2014, ABSTRACT); per-storm scar densities ~23 scars/km² (derived from Fernandes et al. 2004,
ABSTRACT) and 585 scars/1.91 km² affected (Bonini et al. 2025, FULL-A); ~4–5 damaging debris
flows/decade along the range (derived from Cabral et al. 2022, FULL-A: ~7/decade nationally,
"64.5% of the recorded debris-flow events occurred in the Serra do Mar"); Angra dos Reis
civil-defense record 1,640 slides/6 yr (Germain et al. 2021, FULL-A; ≈0.3/km²/yr derived using
~825 km² municipal area — order of magnitude only, occupied-slope bias).

### 3.4 Is lithology the control?

Compilations rank it second-order: Broeckx et al. 2020 (ABSTRACT — retained lithology + topography
+ seismicity, "neither precipitation (intensity) nor vegetation cover were significant"; per-class
coefficients not obtained); Broeckx et al. 2018: "topography is by far the most significant
variable"; Marc et al. 2018 (PARTIAL-A): storm total rainfall first-order, lithology scatter.
Direct metamorphic-vs-igneous comparisons disagree in direction and are ≤2× (Larsen 2012 granite >
volcaniclastic; Broeckx 2019 pyroclastics > basement; Depicker 2021 metamorphic basement = strong
class). **Takeaway: cite envelopes, not a "metamorphic rate"; derive the local rate from our own
pre-event imagery.**
